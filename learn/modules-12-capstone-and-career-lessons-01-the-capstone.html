Lesson 01 — Planning and running your capstone

Time: ~2 hours (then 4–6 weeks of work) · Core

Why a capstone

AdmitFlow was guided: every module told you what to build next. The capstone proves you can do it independently, in a new domain, from a vague problem to a deployed, evaluated, handed-over system. It's also the centrepiece of your portfolio.

Choose well

Pick a brief (or your own client) that:

  • interests you enough to sustain 4–6 weeks
  • matches the specialization you're aiming for (Lesson 05)
  • has a realistic path to real users
  • isn't just AdmitFlow again with different nouns

A six-week plan

WeekLifecycle stageOutputs
1Discover, defineInterviews, process map, problem statement, success metrics, out-of-scope list
2Design, prototypeArchitecture, ADRs, data generated or obtained, a thin end-to-end slice on real/realistic data
3BuildCore features with tests; CI green
4ValidateEvaluation report; security review; fixes
5Deploy, real usersDeployed; real users try it; measure; iterate
6Hand overRunbook, user guide, handover, business impact, recorded presentation

Keep a weekly status update to yourself or a mentor. It's the cheapest way to notice drift.

Scope like an FDE

The commonest capstone failure is building too much and finishing nothing. Write the "won't build" list in week 1, and protect it. A narrow system that works, is evaluated and is used beats a broad demo.

Getting real users

  • Brief D (your own client) has them built in.
  • For A–C: recruit 2–3 people to play roles with realistic tasks (warehouse staff asking safety questions; a "clerk" processing 20 invoices; a "manager" asking 10 questions). Observe them, and measure.
  • Report honestly what happened, including what confused them.

Check yourself

Write your week-1 plan: chosen brief, five discovery questions, the success metric, the "won't build" list, and how you'll get real users.
