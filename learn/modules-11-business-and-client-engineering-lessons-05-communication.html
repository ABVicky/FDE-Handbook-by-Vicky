Lesson 05 — Communicating with stakeholders

Time: ~4 hours · Core

Know your audience

PersonWants to knowFormat
Ms. Rao (director)Is it working? What does it mean for enrolments and the board?One page; three numbers; a decision
Mr. Fernandes (head of admissions)How does my team work day to day? Can I see what's happening?Demo with his team's real data
Ms. Chen (finance)What does it cost, monthly and in total? Any surprises?Table with dates and totals
Mr. Okafor (IT)What do I have to maintain? Is it secure?Runbook, architecture, security review
CounsellorsDoes it make my day easier?Hands-on, at their desk

Same project, five different conversations.

Writing for executives

  • Answer first: the conclusion in the first sentence, then the reasons
  • Three numbers, not thirty, each with a comparison ("52%, up from 45%; target 85%")
  • One clear ask ("approve the extra part-time counsellor by Friday")
  • No jargon: "the system that brings all enquiries into one list", not "the ingestion pipeline"
  • Short: if it doesn't fit on one page, it's not finished

Demos that build trust

From Module 08's Field Track: show real data, show the failure modes and what happens then, and agree measurable acceptance criteria. Rehearse, have a backup (screenshots), and stop talking to let people react.

Explaining technical trade-offs

Make options concrete, with consequences in their terms:

Option A: Google sign-in now. One more week before the team view, and counsellors never need another password. Option B: Team view first. Mr. Fernandes can retire the Friday spreadsheet two weeks sooner; passwords stay for another month. My recommendation: B, because the spreadsheet is what's stopping adoption today.

Saying no (and "not yet")

"I can see why birthday messages would be nice. Doing it now would push the team view back by a week, and that's what's holding adoption back. Could we put it on the list for next term and review it then?"

Acknowledge, explain the trade-off, offer an alternative, let them decide.

Difficult conversations

When things go wrong (late, over budget, a failure), the pattern is the same:

  1. Say it early, before they discover it
  2. Say it plainly: what happened, the impact
  3. Own your part without blaming others
  4. Bring a plan with options and a date
  5. Follow up in writing

Module 11's break & fix reply to Ms. Rao is an example: honest about declaring "done" too early, no blame on counsellors, a specific plan, weekly numbers.

Status updates and meeting notes

Weekly status (template), and after every meeting a short note: decisions, actions (who, by when), open questions. Decisions that aren't written down get re-decided, differently, three weeks later.

Check yourself

Do Exercise 03: write what you'd say in three difficult conversations, then role-play one with a partner who pushes back.
